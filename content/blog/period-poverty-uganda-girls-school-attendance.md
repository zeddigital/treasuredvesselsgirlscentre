## H1: Period Poverty in Uganda: Why Girls Miss School, and What Actually Keeps Them in Class

A teacher in a Ugandan secondary school can often tell you which girls will be missing on any given week. Not because anyone announces it. Because the same desks empty, roughly every month, in a quiet rhythm that nobody talks about.

The register might record it as illness. Sometimes it records nothing at all.

The real reason is simpler, and far more fixable than most people assume. A girl has started her period, and she does not have what she needs to manage it with dignity. So she stays home.

This is period poverty. It is one of the most under-discussed barriers to girls' education in Uganda, and it is also one of the most solvable. Understanding it properly — the scale, the causes, and what the evidence says actually works — matters, because the wrong solution wastes money and the right one changes the direction of a girl's life.

---

## What period poverty actually means

Period poverty is usually described as not being able to afford sanitary products. That definition is true, but it is far too narrow, and the narrowness is part of why so many well-meaning efforts fall short.

UNICEF defines adequate menstrual hygiene management as using a clean material to absorb or collect menstrual blood, being able to change it in private as often as needed, having soap and water to wash, and having somewhere safe to dispose of it.

Read that definition again and notice how much of it has nothing to do with pads.

Privacy. Clean water. Soap. A lockable toilet door. Somewhere to dispose of waste without shame. Knowing what is happening to your own body before it happens. Being able to ask a question without being laughed at.

A girl can be handed a packet of pads and still have almost none of that. This is why period poverty is better understood as a dignity problem than a supply problem. Products are one part of the answer. They are not the whole answer.

---

## The scale of the problem in Uganda

The numbers here are sobering, and they are worth stating carefully.

Research on menstrual health in Uganda has found that only around **10% of adolescent girls practise adequate menstrual hygiene management**, and that girls miss an estimated **11% of school days** because of menstruation.

Eleven per cent sounds abstract. Put it another way: that is roughly one day in every nine of a girl's education, lost to something entirely predictable.

The wider picture is consistent. UNICEF reports that **more than half of adolescent girls in low- and middle-income countries** experience inadequate menstrual hygiene management.

And the educational outcomes tell the end of that story. According to World Bank and UNESCO Institute for Statistics data, only around **25% of Ugandan girls completed lower secondary education** in 2017, with just **4% enrolling in tertiary education** in 2016.

Period poverty is not the sole cause of those figures. Poverty, distance to school, domestic workloads, early marriage and school fees all play their part. But menstruation is a recurring, monthly pressure that pushes in exactly the same direction as all the others — and unlike most of them, it arrives every single month without fail.

---

## Why missing a few days a month changes a girl's whole future

It is tempting to look at a few missed days and think the damage is small. It is not, and the reason is compounding.

A girl who misses several days each month falls behind in class. Falling behind makes lessons harder to follow when she returns. Harder lessons mean lower marks. Lower marks make her feel she is not clever enough to be there. Feeling out of place makes leaving easier.

Very few girls drop out because of a single dramatic event. Most drift out, one manageable absence at a time, until returning feels pointless.

What waits outside the classroom is the harder part. A girl who is no longer in school is significantly more exposed to early marriage and early pregnancy — and Uganda's figures here are stark.

Uganda's Ministry of Health Annual Health Sector Performance Report for 2023/24 recorded a teenage pregnancy rate of **24% nationally** — 21% in urban areas and 25% in rural areas — down slightly from 25% in 2016. UNFPA puts it plainly: around **one in four girls aged 15 to 19 in Uganda has had a child or is pregnant**.

The cost is not only personal. UNFPA estimates that Uganda loses roughly **24% of its potential annually** as a result of adolescent childbearing — in a country where, according to the 2024 National Population and Housing Census, about **73% of the population is under 30**.

That is a national economic question hiding inside what looks like a private, monthly inconvenience.

## The root causes — it is not only about money

If period poverty were purely a matter of cost, the solution would be straightforward. It is not, and the four causes below all need addressing together.

### Cost and availability

Disposable pads are a recurring expense. For a household choosing between food, school fees, medicine and pads, pads lose — every month, reliably. Many girls resort to cloth, tissue, or whatever is to hand, none of which they can trust to hold through a school day.

That lack of trust is the real problem. A girl who is not confident in her protection will not risk sitting in a classroom for six hours.

### Water, toilets and privacy at school

Even with good products, a girl needs somewhere private to change, water to wash with, and a safe way to dispose of waste. Where school sanitation is shared, unlit, lacking a working door lock, or lacking water altogether, staying home is the rational choice.

This is why menstrual health and school sanitation cannot be separated. They are the same issue.

### Silence, stigma and shame

Many girls in Uganda reach their first period knowing nothing about it. The experience arrives as a frightening surprise, sometimes interpreted as illness or injury.

Teasing — often from boys, sometimes from adults — turns a normal biological process into a source of humiliation. Shame is remarkably effective at keeping girls out of classrooms, and it costs nothing to produce.

### Pain that nobody treats

Period pain is routinely dismissed. Yet for some girls it is genuinely disabling, and where simple pain relief is unavailable or never offered, staying home is not avoidance — it is the only option available.

## What actually works — the evidence

Here is the encouraging part. This has been studied properly in Uganda, and the findings are consistent.

The **MENISCUS** research programme, a school-based cluster-randomised trial conducted across 60 secondary schools in Uganda's Wakiso and Kalungu districts, tested a *multi-component* menstrual health intervention rather than a single fix.

The components were:

- **Training teachers** to deliver the government's existing puberty education guidelines properly
- **Training girls** in the use of a menstrual kit and in managing pain
- **Providing pain relief**
- **Improving school water, sanitation and hygiene facilities**
- **A drama skit** to open up conversation and address stigma

The earlier MENISCUS-2 pilot found the approach acceptable and feasible to deliver, with indications of improvement in menstrual-related school absenteeism and in girls' knowledge and management of menstruation.

The lesson matters more than any single statistic: **the components work together, and pads alone are not enough**.

A girl needs a product she trusts, a private place to use it, water to wash, knowledge of what is happening to her body, relief when she is in pain, and a school environment where nobody mocks her for any of it. Remove any one of those and the others weaken.

Two further points are worth drawing out.

**Reusable kits change the economics.** A quality reusable kit removes a recurring monthly cost for around a year. For a household under pressure, that converts an impossible repeating decision into a single manageable one.

**Boys have to be included.** Where menstrual education is delivered only to girls, stigma survives intact in half the classroom. Teaching boys is not a courtesy; it is what makes the school safe enough for girls to return to.

## How Treasured Vessels approaches menstrual dignity in Jinja

At Treasured Vessels Girls' Centre in Jinja, this work sits within our **School Outreach** programme, and we have deliberately built it to match what the evidence supports.

We provide **reusable sanitary kits** that last around a year, rather than a one-off packet that runs out within days. We pair every distribution with **menstrual health education**, so girls understand their own bodies rather than simply receiving a product. We deliver that teaching **to boys as well as girls**, because a classroom where periods are a joke is not a classroom a girl will willingly return to.

And we treat it as connected to everything else we do. A girl kept in school through her teenage years is a girl less exposed to early marriage and early pregnancy. If she has already left school, our vocational training in tailoring, hairdressing, baking and crafts offers another route forward. If she is already a young mother, our teenage mother support meets her where she is.

Menstrual dignity is not a standalone project for us. It is one of the earliest points at which a girl's path can still be changed easily — and changing it early is always cheaper, kinder and more effective than repairing it later.

We are honest about our scale. We are a community-based organisation founded in 2018, running six active programmes with around 30 regular volunteers. We are not a large international agency, and we do not claim to be. What we offer is proximity: we live in this community, we know these families, and we are present long after a distribution day ends.

That belief is at the centre of our work as a Christian organisation. We believe every girl carries a value that her circumstances did not give her and cannot take away.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is period poverty?

Period poverty is the inability to manage menstruation safely and with dignity. It includes not being able to afford sanitary products, but also a lack of clean water, private toilets, soap, safe disposal, pain relief and accurate information about menstruation.

### How many school days do girls in Uganda miss because of their periods?

Research in Uganda has found girls miss an estimated 11% of school days due to menstruation, and that only around 10% of adolescent girls practise adequate menstrual hygiene management.

### Why are reusable sanitary kits better than disposable pads?

A reusable kit lasts around a year, which removes a recurring monthly cost that many households cannot sustain. Disposable pads solve the problem for days; a reusable kit solves it for a year, and produces far less waste in communities with limited disposal facilities.

### Does giving out sanitary pads solve the problem on its own?

No. Evidence from Uganda's MENISCUS trial indicates that effective programmes are multi-component — combining products with education, teacher training, pain relief and improvements to school water and sanitation. Products alone leave most of the barriers in place.

### Why teach boys about menstruation?

Because stigma is a major driver of absenteeism, and teasing frequently comes from male classmates. Educating boys alongside girls reduces shame and helps make the school an environment girls are willing to return to.

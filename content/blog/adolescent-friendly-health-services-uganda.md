## The argument

The brief asks for two things that pull in opposite directions: describe what
good adolescent health care looks like, and be honest that Treasured Vessels does
not provide it. The article resolves that by making the boundary the opening
section rather than a disclaimer at the bottom — the centre is the bridge, not
the destination — and then arguing that the bridge is the part everybody
underestimates.

The spine is **referral completion**. A referral is a chain of roughly nine
links, and handing over a phone number solves none of them. The national ANC
figures make the point without needing to invent anything: contact is nearly
universal, completion is not.

## Sources used

- **WHO — Global standards for quality health care services for adolescents**:
  https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240114012 — the eight standards
  (health literacy; community support; appropriate package of services;
  providers' competencies; facility characteristics; equity and
  non-discrimination; data and quality improvement; adolescents' participation).
  Named as the formal framework; the article's own eight marks are presented
  separately as the experiential version, so WHO is not misquoted.
- **Uganda Ministry of Health — Adolescent Health Policy Guidelines and Service
  Standards (2012)**:
  https://library.health.go.ug/sexual-and-reproductive-health/adolescent-health/adolescent-health-policy-guidelines-and-service
- **Naguru Teenage Information and Health Centre**: https://ntihc.or.ug/about-us/
  — founded 1994, began as an adolescent clinic under Naguru Health Centre, now a
  registered NGO working in adolescent sexual and reproductive health with young
  people aged roughly 10–24 and supporting public facilities.
- **UDHS 2022**: https://www.ubos.org/wp-content/uploads/publications/UDHS-2022-Report.pdf
  — approximately 95% of pregnant women receive ANC from a skilled provider at
  least once; approximately 72% attend four or more visits.
- **WHO — adolescent pregnancy**: mothers aged 10–19 face higher risks of
  eclampsia, puerperal endometritis and systemic infections than mothers aged
  20–24; their babies face higher risks of low birth weight, preterm birth and
  severe neonatal conditions. Phrased as "among the leading causes of death for
  girls aged 15–19 worldwide" rather than "the leading cause", which is the
  older and stronger WHO wording.
- **Ministry of Gender, Labour and Social Development — Uganda Child Helpline 116**:
  https://mglsd.go.ug/uganda-child-helpline-116-2/

## Deliberate omissions

**No Jinja facility is named.** The brief says the centre "should identify and
verify its actual Jinja-area health partners and emergency arrangements" before
publication. That verification has not happened, so rather than omitting the
subject silently the article explains the omission and publishes the five checks
each entry must pass. This converts a gap into a statement of standards, and
gives the centre a defensible position if asked why the directory is empty.

**No unit costs for the referral fund.** The centre's published anchors ($15,
$50, $150) do not cover transport, consultation fees or accompaniment, and
inventing figures would contradict Day Twenty-Eight's argument about transparent
appeals. The article says the costs will be published once verified.

**Age of consent for medical treatment** is raised in the FAQ but not answered,
because it varies by service and facility and we could not verify a single
reliable general answer. It is listed instead as one of the questions being put
to each facility during verification.
